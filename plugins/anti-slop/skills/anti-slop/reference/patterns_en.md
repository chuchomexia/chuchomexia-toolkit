# Telltale patterns in English

Base source: observations on AI-"citable" content patterns (ighenatt) plus
additional notes. This isn't a banned-word list — it's a catalog of what to look
for when a text "reads AI" and you can't immediately say why.

## The em dash contrast

"X is not Y — it is Z." The long dash used to cram a clarification or a rhetorical
pivot into the same sentence is one of the most recognizable tells in generated
English. It shows up because it lets a sentence sound insightful without
committing to a real claim.

- "It's not A — it's B."
- "Not only A, but also B."
- "This isn't just about A — it's about B."

Rule of thumb: if the sentence would work fine with a period or a comma instead
of the dash, use the period or the comma. Save the dash for when the aside is
genuinely parenthetical, not as a rhythm crutch.

## Round, redundant closers

- "In conclusion…" / "Overall…" / "In summary…"
- "The key is…"
- "What matters here is…"
- "In other words…"
- "That said…"
- "It's worth noting that…"

These almost never add new information — they restate what was already said in
slightly different words, as if the reader needed a recap three paragraphs in.
If a piece ends on one of these, try deleting the sentence outright; it usually
reads better without it.

## Overly balanced structure

Sentences of near-identical length, paragraphs that are suspiciously even, lists
where every bullet has the same grammatical shape and word count. This is the
clearest structural tell because real writing has rhythm — short sentences next
to long ones, the occasional fragment. Perfectly symmetric prose is a strong
signal something generated it.

## Inflated, safe vocabulary

Words that show up constantly in generic, hedge-everything AI writing: "crucial",
"significant", "delve", "explore", "navigate", "unlock", "robust", "multifaceted",
"foster", "seamless", "tapestry", "testament", "boasts", "elevate", "leverage"
(as a verb), "landscape" (as in "the X landscape"), "game-changing".

None of these are wrong in isolation — the tell is using them as filler instead
of a more specific, concrete word.

## "Helpful but safe" hedging

Formulations that try to cover every possible case instead of committing to one:
"can vary depending on…", "it's important to consider…", "there are several
factors at play…" used as a way to avoid saying anything falsifiable. Real
writing takes a position, even a modest one.

## Excess transition connectors

"Furthermore,", "Moreover,", "Additionally," opening consecutive paragraphs is
a template signature, not a thinking signature. One transition every so often
is normal prose; one per paragraph is a five-paragraph-essay format bleeding
into everything.

## Rewrite examples

**Before:** "This isn't just a productivity tool — it's a complete workflow
transformation. Not only does it save time, but it also improves output quality.
Overall, the tool represents a significant upgrade to how teams collaborate."

**After:** "The tool doesn't save time by cutting corners — the output actually
gets cleaner, because it forces you to structure the idea before you start
typing."

**Before:** "In conclusion, effective communication is crucial for team success.
It's important to note that clear messaging fosters collaboration and drives
better outcomes."

**After:** "Teams that write clearly tend to argue less about what was actually
agreed."
